# Structuracy
### A game about noticing what sentences do

**Read a sentence. Notice something. See if you can tell the difference.**

That is Structuracy.

It is a game about a familiar human ability that we use every day, usually without noticing that we are using it.

We understand sentences.

We hear somebody speak, read a message, follow a story, listen to an explanation or encounter an unfamiliar idea. Something becomes clear. Something makes sense. We carry that understanding forward.

Most of the time, we pay attention to what the sentence is about.

Structuracy invites us to notice something else.

**What did the sentence actually do to make itself understandable?**

Every sentence gives us something we can grasp. It might establish a difference, make a possibility available, show how things depend on one another, put something right, bring separate things together, or bring an unanswered matter to rest.

These are familiar experiences. We don't ordinarily need special names for them.

But when we put two sentences beside each other and pay close attention, we begin to notice remarkably precise differences between the ways they make themselves understood.

Structuracy is a game designed to help people discover those differences for themselves.

You don't need to know anything about language theory.

You don't need to memorise complicated terms.

You only need to read, notice, choose and gradually become more precise about what you can sense.

---

## 1. The idea behind the game

Consider these three sentences:

**The door is closed.**

**The door closed.**

**The door can now be closed.**

All three concern the same door. All three are easily understood.

Yet they do not do quite the same thing.

One establishes a condition.

Another tells us that something happened and reached an end.

The third makes an ability or possibility available.

We can sense these differences without having to explain them.

That is our starting point.

Structuracy gives us opportunities to examine such distinctions until we become increasingly confident about what separates one kind of sentence from another.

The challenge is not to decide which sentence is more interesting, more beautiful, more useful or more truthful.

It is to notice exactly what each sentence makes understandable.

The subject of the sentence might be a door, a mountain, a broken bicycle, a mathematical theorem, a family disagreement or a distant galaxy.

The subject can change completely.

The kind of understanding the sentence makes possible may remain recognisably the same.

And two sentences about the same subject may accomplish very different things.

**Structuracy asks us to attend to those things.**

---

## 2. Three questions behind every sentence

The game is built around three very ordinary questions.

### What becomes different?

When a sentence has been understood, something has become available to us that wasn't available in quite the same way before.

Perhaps we can now distinguish two possibilities.

Perhaps we recognise a way forward.

Perhaps we understand how two things depend on each other.

Perhaps something that was wrong has been put right.

Perhaps separate parts have become one organised whole.

Or perhaps a question that was still waiting for an answer no longer needs one.

The first question asks us to notice the kind of change the sentence makes understandable.

### What makes it enough?

A sentence must arrive somewhere.

Sometimes everything comes down to one particular point.

Sometimes the result only becomes clear when we take a whole set, sequence or arrangement into account.

And sometimes the decisive event is that something which previously governed the situation no longer has that power.

The second question asks us to notice **what makes the sentence's achievement sufficient**.

### What do we have afterward?

Once we have understood a sentence, its achievement is available to us in some way.

Sometimes we simply have a definite result.

Sometimes what we have depends upon a comparison, condition, proportion, source or connection.

Sometimes what we have makes something possible, guides what happens next or changes how something can work.

The third question asks us to notice **the way the result continues to matter**.

These three questions belong together.

They are not three separate things that happen one after another.

They are three ways of paying attention to the same single achievement.

---

## 3. Six familiar kinds of change

The first question—*what becomes different?*—has six broad possibilities.

### 1. Something becomes distinguishable

What was unclear, mixed together or easily confused becomes definite enough for a difference to matter.

We can now tell one thing from another.

Perhaps we identify the right person, notice a boundary or recognise that two apparently similar things are not actually the same.

The important achievement is that a difference has become clear.

### 2. A way becomes available

Something that was previously out of reach becomes reachable.

An option appears. A path becomes available. There is somewhere to go, something to try or some way to continue.

The important achievement is a greater range of possibilities.

### 3. A connection becomes understandable

The sentence shows how things depend on, correspond to, answer to, affect or determine one another.

The important achievement is not simply that several things exist.

It is that how they go together has become understandable.

A comparison, a sequence, a proportion or a mutual dependence might be essential.

### 4. Something is put right

An arrangement that wasn't working, fitting or meeting what was required now does.

Something is corrected.

The important achievement is that the fault or mismatch no longer determines the result.

This might concern a physical object, a calculation, an arrangement or a way of proceeding.

### 5. Separate parts become one organised whole

Things that were separate acquire places or roles through belonging together.

The important achievement is not merely that several items are present.

It is that their organisation gives us a whole whose parts have a place in it.

That whole may have a coherence, strength or ability that the separate pieces did not have.

### 6. Something no longer needs an answer

A matter that was waiting for a decision, explanation, response or further action has reached a sufficient end.

The demand for something more has been answered or has ceased to govern what happens.

The important achievement is that something formerly left open no longer remains pending in the same way.

These six possibilities concern different kinds of intelligible change.

The game helps us become sensitive to those differences without requiring us to learn a list of names.

---

## 4. Three ways a sentence can become enough

Now consider the second question.

*What makes it enough?*

There are three possibilities.

### 1. Everything comes down to one particular point

A single decisive place, item, answer, connection or turning point gives the sentence what it needs.

Other things may be mentioned, but one particular point is essential to why the result is enough.

### 2. The whole spread matters

The sentence cannot achieve what it does through one isolated point.

The result needs a range, a sequence, a collection of cases, several positions or a network of things considered together.

It is the wider arrangement that makes the result enough.

### 3. Something loses its former hold

The sentence achieves what it does because something that previously determined the situation no longer determines it.

This is not simply about removing an object or using a negative word.

What matters is that an earlier rule, condition, demand or constraint has lost its authority over the result.

These are three different ways in which a sentence can arrive at something sufficient.

---

## 5. Three ways the result remains available

The third question asks:

*What do we have afterward?*

Again, there are three possibilities.

### 1. We have a definite result

A determination has been made.

Something is now identifiable, established, decided or clear.

The achievement itself is what remains available.

### 2. We have something that holds through a connection

The result depends on how one thing compares with, answers to, corresponds with or depends upon another.

The connection isn't merely an interesting detail.

It is essential to what the result is.

Take that connection away, and we no longer have the same achievement.

### 3. We have something that makes a difference to what can happen

The achievement has practical force within the situation.

Something can now happen, work, proceed, guide a decision or bring about a consequence in a way that matters.

The important thing is not an unrelated event happening later.

It is that the sentence has made an ability, effect or changed course of action understandable as the result.

These are three ways in which an intelligible achievement remains available once the sentence has done its work.

---

## 6. Where the 54 comes from

We now have:

- Six possibilities for what becomes different.
- Three possibilities for what makes the achievement enough.
- Three possibilities for what remains available afterward.

That gives us:

**6 × 3 × 3 = 54**

These are the 54 possibilities at the heart of Structuracy.

But this is not a system for attaching three unrelated labels to a sentence.

Each possibility is one particular kind of achievement.

The three questions shape that achievement together.

What becomes different matters because of what makes it enough and how its result remains available.

What makes it enough matters because of the particular change involved and the way that change continues to count.

And what remains available is the result of that very change arriving in that very way.

**Every one of the 54 is a complete possibility in its own right.**

A sentence might be about friendship, medicine, mathematics, cooking, music or a storm.

Those subjects do not determine which possibility it belongs to.

What matters is what the sentence actually accomplishes.

The game is designed around the possibility that these 54 ways of making something understandable are available to us in ordinary language all the time.

---

## 7. The importance of the almost-right answer

There is something fascinating about sentences.

When we understand one, we are usually occupied by its meaning.

We understand what is being said.

We rarely notice the nearby alternatives that would have made the sentence do something slightly different.

Yet sometimes the best way to recognise what a sentence actually accomplishes is to notice what it comes remarkably close to accomplishing—but does not.

Consider:

**The whole system is working again.**

We understand it immediately.

Now ask:

Does the sentence chiefly establish that the arrangement is correct?

Or does it establish that the system has regained its ability to work?

Both descriptions concern closely connected possibilities.

But they are not identical.

The second is more directly expressed by the sentence.

That small distinction is exactly the kind of thing Structuracy brings to our attention.

A good question does not require a ridiculous wrong answer.

It offers another possibility that is genuinely plausible.

We have to look carefully enough to recognise why one account fits better than the other.

And when we can explain the difference, something interesting happens.

We become more precise about an ability we already possessed.

**The almost-right answer gives us traction on the right one.**

## 8. Game One: What Does This Sentence Really Do?

The first game presents one sentence.

The player reads it.

Then the game asks a question about what the sentence accomplishes.

For example:

**The whole system is working again.**

*Which of these does the sentence make clearest?*

A. The system has regained its ability to work.

B. The arrangement of its parts now meets the required standard.

C. The parts now hold together through their mutual dependence.

All three possibilities make sense as kinds of understanding.

But one describes the actual sentence more directly.

The player chooses.

The game then gives a short explanation showing what matters in the wording.

It may also explain why a tempting alternative doesn't quite fit.

The purpose is not to trap the player.

It is to help the player notice something that was there to be noticed, but that ordinary reading might have passed over.

### What makes a good question?

A good question draws attention to a genuine difference.

Its alternatives must represent real possibilities, not meaningless distractions.

The question should reward attention to what the sentence actually says.

It should not reward guessing based on a particular keyword.

It should not assume that a word always performs the same job.

And it should not invent an interpretation that requires information the sentence hasn't supplied.

Some questions will be easy.

Others will require careful attention.

The most rewarding ones may be those where two answers initially seem equally attractive, but a closer reading makes the decisive difference clear.

### The unseen alternative

Every sentence can be considered alongside nearby possibilities.

Some are quite far away from what it accomplishes.

Others are remarkably close.

The game is especially interested in those close alternatives.

They help us discover what is essential to the sentence by drawing attention to what would need to be different for another account to become the better one.

The player gradually learns to recognise not only what a sentence says, but what it does **instead of doing something else**.

---

## 9. Game Two: Spot the Difference

The second game presents two sentences.

The player reads the first sentence and then the second.

The question is always some version of:

**What does the second sentence change about the first?**

This game has one absolute rule.

**Only one of the three underlying questions may have a different answer.**

If the two sentences differ in the kind of change they make understandable, the other two aspects remain the same.

If they differ in what makes the achievement enough, the other two aspects remain the same.

And if they differ in how the result remains available, the other two aspects remain the same.

That gives the player a precise task.

We are not asking for every difference in wording, imagery, subject matter or ordinary meaning.

We are asking for the one difference that matters to the game's underlying structure.

For example:

**Sentence A:** Now I can tell which one it is.

**Sentence B:** The difference shows up in how these two compare.

*What does the second sentence change about the first?*

A. The difference now depends upon a comparison.

B. The difference now changes what can happen next.

C. The difference now needs an entire collection to become clear.

The player chooses the account that most precisely describes what has changed.

Both sentences make something distinguishable.

Both bring their achievement to a particular point.

But they differ in how the achieved distinction remains available.

One gives us a definite identification.

The other makes the comparison essential to the difference being established.

That is the distinction the player is invited to recognise.

And through repeated questions like this, the player gradually becomes better at identifying exactly what has changed—and what has not.

---

## 10. Why the choices matter

The multiple-choice answers are not merely a way to score the game.

**They are part of how the game teaches.**

Every answer is written using everyday descriptions of the possibilities the game is built around.

This matters enormously.

Suppose a question offers these alternatives:

- Something becomes clear at one decisive point.
- Something becomes clear across a whole range.
- Something becomes clear because an earlier constraint no longer determines the result.

All three are meaningful possibilities.

Only one may correctly describe the sentence being examined.

But simply encountering these alternatives gives the player another opportunity to recognise the differences between them.

The same descriptions will recur in different settings, with different sentences.

Over time, their meanings become increasingly familiar.

The player begins to notice them without needing to recall a definition.

Eventually, the player may be able to say:

*“No, this isn't really about all the parts taken together. The sentence turns on this one particular point.”*

Or:

*“The result isn't just that something has been decided. The decision now makes something possible.”*

Or:

*“The connection isn't incidental. Without it, this would be a different kind of statement.”*

That is precisely the ability the game seeks to cultivate.

The player is acquiring an everyday language for distinctions that were previously difficult to express.

---

## 11. How many different questions can the game ask?

The 54 possibilities give us a precise way to work out every comparison in which only one underlying aspect differs.

There are:

**135 comparisons** involving different kinds of change while the other two aspects remain the same.

**54 comparisons** involving different ways of making the achievement enough while the other two aspects remain the same.

**54 comparisons** involving different ways the result remains available while the other two aspects remain the same.

Together, these give us:

**243 distinct one-change comparisons.**

These 243 comparisons form the complete foundation for Game Two.

They also provide a rich source of nearby alternatives for Game One.

Each of the 54 possibilities has nine immediate neighbours in this arrangement.

But not every neighbouring possibility will be equally tempting for every sentence.

The game's questions should pay attention to the particular sentence and choose alternatives that make the player think.

### The first journey

The first learning journey can introduce every one of the 243 comparisons.

A later journey can present them in the opposite direction.

Further journeys can use different sentences to revisit the same differences.

The player may encounter an apparently familiar challenge but discover that a different sentence makes the distinction harder to recognise.

This allows the game to become progressively more demanding without continually inventing new rules.

The underlying possibilities are already known.

The variety comes from the countless ways ordinary sentences can express them.

## 12. The sentence cartridges

Structuracy is designed like a simple game console.

The console knows how the game works.

The cartridge supplies the sentences.

Think of an old home computer or games machine.

You have the machine itself.

Then you insert a cartridge and enter a different world.

Structuracy follows that idea.

The website provides the playing experience.

It displays sentences, presents choices, responds to answers, keeps score and guides the player through the available challenges.

A separate cartridge contains the sentences.

Each sentence has an assigned place among the 54 possibilities.

The player doesn't have to see that assignment.

The machine uses it to find suitable questions.

### A cartridge knows where its sentences belong

Imagine a cartridge containing 540 sentences, with ten sentences assigned to each of the 54 possibilities.

The console can examine those assignments and discover which sentences can be paired for a one-change question.

It does not need someone to write a completely new quiz for every possible pair.

The underlying comparison is already known.

The machine can draw on its library of carefully written questions and answers, select appropriate sentences and assemble the round.

A new cartridge can contain entirely different subject matter while working in the same way.

### Different cartridges, same game

A cartridge could contain:

**Everyday life:** ordinary conversations, situations, explanations and observations.

**Science:** statements about physical, chemical and biological relationships.

**Mathematics:** statements of principles, relationships, conditions and proofs.

**Stories:** sentences drawn from fiction, fables and fairy tales.

**Poetry:** language that asks for especially delicate attention.

**Other languages:** collections that allow players to encounter the same underlying possibilities through different languages.

**Advanced challenges:** sentences where the nearest alternatives are especially difficult to distinguish.

The console does not need to be rebuilt for each new collection.

The cartridge changes.

The playing experience remains familiar.

---

## 13. What the machine knows and what the player sees

Behind the scenes, Structuracy has a detailed reference system.

It knows all 54 possibilities.

It knows which ones can be compared by changing just one aspect.

It knows the appropriate contrasts between those possibilities.

It can draw on a carefully prepared library of everyday descriptions to construct questions and provide explanations.

The player, however, does not need to see any of that machinery.

The player sees:

A sentence.

Or two sentences.

A question.

A small number of choices.

A brief explanation.

Then another round.

That is enough.

The game should feel simple because the machine already knows what it is trying to draw attention to.

The complexity belongs behind the screen.

The experience belongs to the player.

---

## 14. Where new sentences come from

The game can begin with an existing collection of 540 sentences spread across all 54 possibilities.

It does not need to wait for thousands of additional sentences before becoming playable.

But Structuracy is also designed to grow.

Its underlying framework allows us to ask for fresh examples of any one of the 54 possibilities.

A dedicated sentence-making instrument can begin with the intended kind of achievement and produce new ordinary expressions that embody it.

This instrument is not limited to any particular subject.

It can work with situations involving everyday life, science, mathematics, personal experience or anything else that can be expressed in a sentence.

The same underlying possibility may be expressed through many entirely different kinds of content.

That creates a path toward a very large collection without requiring every sentence to be invented separately by hand.

The sentence-making instrument belongs to the project's internal toolkit.

It is not something the player needs to see or use.

The game itself should remain playable without asking an AI to invent or judge every answer while a round is being played.

---

## 15. What progress should feel like

The aim is not to make players memorise 54 numbered categories.

The aim is to help them become increasingly accurate in noticing what sentences accomplish.

A beginner might recognise a fairly obvious contrast.

One sentence makes a fact clear.

Another shows that the fact changes what can happen.

An improving player might recognise a much smaller difference.

Two sentences both make a connection understandable, but one does so through a single decisive point while another requires a whole arrangement.

A skilled player might recognise why two very similar accounts are not equally faithful to a sentence.

They might be able to explain which part of the wording matters and what would need to change for the other account to fit.

An accomplished player can encounter unfamiliar sentences and articulate the difference in clear, ordinary language.

They do not need to quote a rule.

They do not need to produce a number.

They can simply tell you what one sentence does that the other does not.

**That is mastery in Structuracy.**

It means becoming precise about something that previously happened almost automatically.

### Learning without feeling lectured

The game should never feel like an examination in specialist vocabulary.

Its questions should be interesting in themselves.

Its explanations should be satisfying rather than lengthy.

Its difficulty should grow naturally.

And its answers should make players think:

*“Ah, yes. That's exactly the difference.”*

The most satisfying moment is not merely being told that a choice was correct.

It is recognising why the other possibilities were not quite right.

That feeling of recognition is central to the experience.

---

## 16. What makes a fair round?

The game must be careful.

A question should never be presented as having a perfectly obvious answer when the wording genuinely supports more than one account.

A sentence must be sufficiently clear for the distinction being tested.

The choices must be faithful to the real possibilities available within the game's framework.

In the two-sentence game, the selected sentences must belong to positions that differ in exactly one of the three underlying respects.

And the explanation should identify the actual difference rather than merely repeating the correct option.

These are important qualities of the game.

They allow the questions to be subtle without being arbitrary.

They also create a useful distinction between an easy question and an unfair one.

An easy question contains a difference that is simple to notice.

A difficult question contains a difference that demands attention.

An unfair question asks the player to know something the sentences do not make sufficiently clear.

Structuracy should strive for difficulty, never unfairness.

## 17. A game that can keep growing

The first version can be very small.

One website.

Two ways to play.

One sentence cartridge.

A set of carefully prepared choices and explanations.

A score.

A next-round button.

That is enough to begin.

Over time, the game can introduce daily challenges, levels, personal progress, different cartridges and different languages.

Players may choose to explore a particular collection or work through a carefully arranged series of comparisons.

Some may simply enjoy playing a few rounds.

Others may become fascinated by the subtlety of the distinctions.

Some may want to master every possible comparison.

The game should accommodate all of them without changing its central character.

**Read. Notice. Choose. Understand a little more precisely.**

## 18. Why this might matter beyond the game

Human beings spend much of their lives making sense of sentences.

We use them to learn, reason, disagree, decide, explain, apologise, persuade, discover and cooperate.

Yet understanding usually feels immediate.

We pay attention to the people, objects, events and ideas being discussed.

We rarely examine the way a sentence makes those things understandable.

Structuracy begins from the possibility that this familiar human activity has a recognisable underlying order.

The game offers a way to explore that possibility through direct experience.

It does not ask players to believe a theory before playing.

It asks them to notice distinctions and decide for themselves whether they can recognise them.

A player might become better at reading carefully.

They might become more precise when explaining why two statements differ.

They might discover that very different subjects can be expressed through surprisingly similar kinds of understanding.

Or they might simply enjoy a game that makes them look at ordinary sentences in a new way.

All of those are worthwhile outcomes.

The greater ambition is that the ability being practised could become part of everyday intellectual life.

Not because everybody learns a technical vocabulary.

But because people become more sensitive to differences they have always been capable of recognising.

---

## 19. The central promise

Structuracy is a simple game with a carefully worked-out structure beneath it.

The player does not need to know the machinery.

The player does not need to study a textbook.

The player does not need to be interested in linguistics.

The player only needs to notice that one sentence accomplishes something slightly different from another.

The game gives that difference a shape.

The choices give the player language for it.

The explanation makes it clearer.

And the next round provides another opportunity to recognise it.

Over time, these small acts of attention may develop into a much more precise way of reading, noticing and explaining.

There are 54 possibilities at the heart of the game.

There are 243 fundamental comparisons in which exactly one aspect changes.

There can be thousands upon thousands of sentences through which to explore them.

But the experience itself remains beautifully simple.

**One sentence. One question. One distinction worth noticing.**

Or:

**Two sentences. One difference. Can you say exactly what it is?**

That is Structuracy.

**See what sentences do.**
